I have worked with records of marine species contributed by divers, fishers and the public. They are useful, and they have limits, and it helps to know where the one ends and the other begins.

## What they are good at

**Coverage.** Divers, fishers and the public are in more places, for more of the year, than any survey team. For species that spread faster than formal surveys can follow, their observations may be the first sign.

**First records.** A single well-documented sighting is evidence that a species is present somewhere it was not known to be. A photograph can be checked by a taxonomist, which turns an anecdote into a record. Many published first records rest on observations of this kind.

**Range changes.** A series of records, checked and dated, shows where a species has been found over time.

## What they are not good at

**Absence.** A species not reported was not necessarily absent. Records show presence, and the lack of a record may mean nobody looked, or looked and could not see it.

**Effort.** Reports come from where people dive, fish and have a phone. They bunch around harbours and popular dive sites, in summer, in the daytime. Without a record of the effort behind them, a density is a map of observers.

**Conspicuousness.** A large, colourful or unusual animal gets reported much more readily than a small, cryptic one. Trends in reports can then follow the attention a species receives, not its abundance.

**Identification.** Similar species are confused, and the confusion is not random. Verification by experts, and photographs wherever possible, limit the damage.

## From records to numbers

Turning records into quantities needs a model of how the records arose. The central idea is imperfect detection: a species can be present and unseen, and the probability of seeing it depends on effort, observer and conditions. Occupancy models, developed by MacKenzie and colleagues in the early 2000s, use repeated visits to estimate the detection probability and the occupancy together. Without repeated visits or a measure of effort, the two are confounded, which is the identifiability problem of an earlier post.

Practical steps follow.

- Record effort whenever possible: how long, how many observers, where, which gear.
- Record non-detections, not only detections. A "nothing seen" report is worth a great deal.
- Keep a verification step, and record who verified.
- Compare against a structured survey in a few places, to calibrate.
- Be explicit about what the numbers mean: reports per observer-hour is not density.

## A matter of method

Collecting records is data-gathering and monitoring, not modelling. It gives a respect for the people who collect the data and a wariness about what the data can carry. A model fitted to records without regard to the effort behind them can produce a confident answer to the wrong question.

Collecting the records is also a form of public engagement. Participants who know why effort matters tend to report better, and that makes a better dataset.
