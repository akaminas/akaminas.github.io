I work on both ecological and socioeconomic systems, and people sometimes ask whether that's two fields. For modelling it's closer to one. This first post is about why, and where the resemblance stops.

## What carries over

The parts differ. Organisms vary in size, traits and history, and so do firms, households and organisations. A model of the average member of either group tends to miss what matters, which is often the spread: a few large or unusual members and a long tail of small ones. A quick way to see how much the average hides: take sizes that are lognormal with a standard deviation of the logarithm of 1.5. The mean is 3.1 times the median, the top 10 per cent hold 59 per cent of the total and the bottom half holds 7 per cent. (That's from the formula, and a sample of two million agrees.) About 77 per cent of the members are smaller than the mean. Firm sizes are strongly right-skewed and sizes within a community of organisms often are too, though how much varies a lot.

Interactions feed back. Prey support predators and predators reduce prey. The same pair of equations, Lotka and Volterra's, turns up in Goodwin's 1967 model of cycles in employment and the wage share: high employment raises wages, higher wages squeeze profit and investment, and employment then falls. Nobody claims an economy is a pond. The claim is only that two different systems can share a loop, and a loop has consequences whatever it's made of. Linearise either pair around its equilibrium and you get the same 2 x 2 matrix: zeros on the diagonal, and off-diagonal entries of opposite sign. Its eigenvalues are purely imaginary, so near the equilibrium you get cycles, with period 2 pi / sqrt(pq) if p and q are the sizes of the two off-diagonal entries. (The closed cycles belong to the textbook form and are fragile. In the Lotka-Volterra case, give the prey a little self-limitation and they damp.)

Things happen at different speeds. A fish stock recovers over years or decades, a budget is set every year, a law takes a parliament. When a fast process sits on top of a slow one, much of the difficulty is deciding which of them to treat as already settled.

There are thresholds. A shallow lake can be clear or turbid at the same nutrient load, and an institution can be hard to leave once everyone has adapted to it. A small change at the wrong moment can move the whole system in either case, and the way back can be a different path from the way in. A later post takes this up under the name of catastrophe theory. For now, a number. Take the usual shallow-lake phosphorus model, dP/dt = L - sP + rP^q / (m^q + P^q), where L is the loading and the last term is recycling that switches on sharply around P = m (Carpenter, Ludwig and Brock 1999 use this form). I set s = r = m = 1 and q = 8, which is arbitrary apart from giving two states. For loadings between about 0.39 and 0.66 the lake has two stable states, at L = 0.5 either P = 0.50 or P = 1.45. Raise the loading slowly from the clear state and nothing happens until about 0.66, when it jumps. To get back you have to bring the loading under about 0.39.

Noise and history matter. Two identical starts can end differently, and rare events leave long marks. The simplest case I know is a Polya urn: start with one red and one blue ball, draw one at random, put it back with another of the same colour, and repeat. The red fraction settles, but at a random value, and that value is uniform between 0 and 1. (I ran 20,000 urns of 2,000 draws each: about 20 per cent finished below 0.2 and 20 per cent above 0.8.) Arthur (1989) modelled lock-in between competing technologies with a process in the same family, where each adoption makes the next one of the same kind more likely. The ecological version is a priority effect, where whoever arrives first tends to hold the site. Both fields mostly work with observation rather than experiment, on a single history that can't be rerun, so the models have to do some of the work that a control group would do elsewhere.

Measurement is a problem of its own. Counting species isn't the same as knowing what a community does, since which species are present matters as well as how many. Take two communities of ten species. In one every species has 10 per cent of the individuals. In the other one species has 91 per cent and the other nine have 1 per cent each. Richness is 10 in both. The exponential of the Shannon index, an effective number of species, is 10 in the first and 1.65 in the second. A single index of an organisation's sustainability has the same problem: it depends on what went into it and how it was weighted. Both fields compress many dimensions into one number and then forget the choices that were made.

## Where the resemblance stops

People anticipate. A policy changes the behaviour it was designed from, which is the core of what economists call the Lucas critique. The usual example is the trade-off between inflation and unemployment, which looked stable in past data and then stopped being so once policy leaned on it. Organisms don't read the paper. They do respond to what's done to them, only without foresight and more slowly: Law (2000) reviews how fishing can select for earlier maturation, and how much of that happens in real stocks is still argued over. So the difference is anticipation, and not a feedback from policy to system. A model of a social system is sometimes part of the system it describes, and that has no close counterpart in a population of fish.

Goals are contested. Ecology has a common currency in fitness, even if it's hard to measure. Social systems have competing objectives, and an optimum for one group is a loss for another. Take the textbook fishery: logistic growth, with catch proportional to effort times stock. Write effort as a fraction e of the stock's intrinsic growth rate. The equilibrium stock is K(1 - e) and the yield is rK e (1 - e), which peaks at e = 0.5 with the stock at half of K. Now add a cost of effort, set so that a unit of effort at the unfished stock would earn five times its cost. Profit peaks at e = 0.4, with the stock at 0.6 K and a yield 96 per cent of the maximum. An open-access fleet keeps entering until profit is zero, which happens at e = 0.8, with the stock down at 0.2 K and the yield at 64 per cent of the maximum (Gordon 1954 is the classic paper on the economics). Same model, three answers, depending on whose objective you take. The model can say what follows from each choice. It can't make the choice.

The default reasoning differs. Economics leans towards equilibrium, ecology towards dynamics. I find it useful to say early whether a result describes a steady state or a transient, because a reader trained in the other tradition will otherwise assume the opposite.

## What I take from it

What transfers best is the habit of asking the same questions of any system. What are the state variables? Which feedbacks are positive and which negative? On what timescales do they act? Where might thresholds sit? What can actually be observed?

The equations themselves, and the assumptions that came with them, shouldn't be transferred without checking. A logistic curve describes a population limited by food and a market limited by buyers, but the reasons for the limit differ, and so does what happens when it's reached.

The posts after this one take the questions one at a time: how to think about a model in the first place, what equilibria and noise do to each other, why dimensions matter, and how to report the results honestly.
