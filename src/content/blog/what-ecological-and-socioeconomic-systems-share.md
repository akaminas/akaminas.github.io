I trained as a marine scientist, I now build models of ecological communities, and part of my work is on organisations and sustainability. That can look like two fields. For the purposes of modelling it is closer to one, and this first post says why, and where the resemblance stops.

## What carries over

**The parts differ.** Organisms differ in size, traits and history. Firms, households and organisations differ too. A model of the average member of either group tends to miss what matters, which is often the spread: a few large or unusual members, and a long tail of small ones.

**Interactions feed back.** Prey support predators and predators reduce prey. The same pair of equations, Lotka and Volterra's, turns up in Goodwin's 1967 model of cycles in employment and the wage share: a high share of employment raises wages, higher wages squeeze profit and investment, and employment then falls. Nobody claims an economy is a pond. The point is that two different systems can share a loop, and a loop has consequences whatever it is made of.

**Things happen at different speeds.** A fish stock recovers over years or decades, a budget is set every year, a law takes a parliament. When a fast process sits on top of a slow one, much of the difficulty is deciding which to treat as already settled.

**There are thresholds.** A shallow lake can be clear or turbid at the same nutrient load, and an institution can be hard to leave once everyone has adapted to it. In both cases a small change at the wrong moment can move the whole system, and the way back can be a different path from the way in. A later post takes this up under the name of catastrophe theory.

**Noise and history matter.** Two identical starts can end differently, and rare events leave long marks. Both fields mostly work with observation rather than experiment, on a single history that cannot be rerun, so the models have to do some of the work that a control group would do elsewhere.

**Measurement is a problem of its own.** Counting species is not the same as knowing what a community does, since which species are present matters as well as how many. In the same way a single index of an organisation's sustainability depends on what went into it and how it was weighted. Both fields compress many dimensions into one number and then forget the choices that were made.

## Where the resemblance stops

**People anticipate.** A policy changes the behaviour it was designed from, which is the core of what economists call the Lucas critique. Organisms do not read the paper. A model of a social system is therefore sometimes part of the system it describes, and that has no real counterpart in a population of fish.

**Goals are contested.** Ecology has a common currency in fitness, even if it is hard to measure. Social systems have competing objectives, and an optimum for one group is a loss for another. A model can say what follows from a choice, but the choice is not its to make.

**The default reasoning differs.** Economics leans towards equilibrium, ecology towards dynamics. I find it useful to say early whether a result describes a steady state or a transient, because a reader trained in the other tradition will otherwise assume the opposite.

## What I take from it

What transfers best is the habit of asking the same questions of any system. What are the state variables? Which feedbacks are positive and which negative? On what timescales do they act? Where might thresholds sit? What can actually be observed?

What should not be transferred without checking is the equations themselves, and the assumptions that came with them. A logistic curve describes a population limited by food and a market limited by buyers, but the reasons for the limit differ, and so does what happens when it is reached.

The posts that follow take these questions one at a time: how to think about a model in the first place, what equilibria and noise do to each other, why dimensions matter, and how to report the results honestly.
