A pasture, a fishery or an irrigation canal is shared, and each user gains by taking a little more than their share, whatever the others do. Followed through, that logic ends with the resource used up. Often it is not.

## The argument and the evidence

Hardin (1968) set out the logic in "The tragedy of the commons". Each herder on a shared pasture gains the full benefit of one more animal and bears only a share of the cost of the extra grazing, so each adds animals and the pasture is overgrazed. Ostrom (1990), in *Governing the Commons*, collected cases that run the other way: alpine pastures, forests and irrigation systems managed by their users, some of them for centuries, along with others that failed. She found features that the long-lived arrangements tended to share. They included clear boundaries around who may use the resource, monitoring by the users or by people answerable to them, sanctions that start small and grow with repeated breaches, and cheap ways of settling disputes.

## A model with two strategies

Take a large population in which each user either cooperates, keeping to the agreed take, or defects, taking more. Let x be the share of cooperators. Users meet at random and receive payoffs, and a strategy that earns more than average becomes more common, because it is copied or because it reproduces. This is the replicator equation (Taylor and Jonker 1978): the share changes at the rate x (1 - x) times the payoff to cooperating minus the payoff to defecting.

Two games, with these payoffs to a user:

| Your choice, the other's choice | Game 1 | Game 2 |
|---|---|---|
| cooperate, cooperate | 3 | 4 |
| cooperate, defect | 0 | 0 |
| defect, cooperate | 5 | 3 |
| defect, defect | 1 | 2 |

In game 1 defecting pays more whatever the other user does, which makes it a dilemma. In game 2 cooperating pays when the other cooperates and defecting pays when the other defects, so there is a payoff for coordinating.

## What happens

In game 1 the share of cooperators falls to zero from any start, which is Hardin's result. In game 2 full cooperation and full defection are both stable, and the two payoffs are equal at a share of two thirds. A population that starts above two thirds cooperators ends with all of them cooperating, and one that starts below ends with none.

That cooperation can persist in game 2 does not mean it will. Both ends are stable, so where the population starts decides. One way to read Ostrom's design principles is as ways of moving a community from game 1 to game 2, so that keeping to the rule pays when most others keep to it. That is my reading, not her claim, and a model with four payoffs cannot say which rules do it. There is also a third case, in which cooperators and defectors coexist, which I have not drawn.

## The same mathematics elsewhere

The equation is also used for microbes that secrete a molecule others can use, where x is the share of cells and the payoffs are growth rates, and for people choosing whether to keep to a rule, where x is the share of users and the payoffs are what they gain. A shared equation does not make the mechanisms alike: cells do not deliberate, and people do.

## Limits

The population is large, well mixed and without memory. Users meet at random, once, with fixed payoffs. Real users meet again, know one another and can change the rules, and these are the features Ostrom emphasised. The model shows what the payoffs would have to look like for cooperation to persist, and says nothing about whether real commons have them.

## Questions for a shared resource

- Who may use it?
- Can use be seen by the others?
- What happens to someone who takes too much, and who decides?
- How cheaply are disputes settled?

Each answer changes a payoff in the table.
