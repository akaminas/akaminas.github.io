Sustainability is usually discussed in the language of values and commitments, and those matter. But the questions that decide whether something is sustainable are about trade-offs, thresholds, delays and feedback, and each of those is quantitative. A claim with no quantity in it cannot be wrong, which is a weakness.

## Terms that carry numbers

A handful of ideas from the field show how much hangs on a number.

**The rebound effect.** Efficiency gains are partly offset by increased use, an old observation associated with Jevons and coal in the nineteenth century. Whether a more efficient process lowers total impact depends on how much extra use it invites, which is a number.

**Relative and absolute decoupling.** Impact per unit of output can fall while total impact rises, if output grows faster. The two statements are both true of some economies, and only the second concerns the climate or the sea.

**The discount rate.** How future costs and benefits are weighed against present ones can dominate every other assumption in an analysis. It belongs in the main text, not an appendix, with the result shown under an alternative.

**System boundaries and functional units.** A life-cycle assessment compares products per kilogram, per use or per year of service. Change the unit and the ranking of alternatives can change. A favourable result often reflects a convenient boundary.

**Planetary boundaries.** These are global thresholds. A single firm cannot stay within them, and a firm-level model cannot claim to.

## Commitment and practice

My co-authors and I studied how fifty Greek environmental organisations committed to sustainable development and circular-economy principles. We built an index from public information and read it through three frameworks from organisational sociology. The finding was that commitment is predominantly conceptual, with gradual efforts to turn it into concrete practice.

That is a cross-section: one moment, fifty organisations. It cannot say how quickly stated commitment becomes practice, whether the gap is closing, or whether it stalls. Those are questions about rates, and answering them needs a model with the two quantities as state variables and data over time. The same applies to many sustainability claims. A snapshot shows a gap, and only dynamics show whether it is narrowing.

## Where the numbers are thin

There is a limit to this argument, and it is worth stating plainly. Reviews in the field note that environmental assessment is mature and social assessment is not. A dynamic model of material and environmental flows does not touch that gap. A study that models two of the three pillars should say so, since claiming three while modelling two is the kind of overreach a careful reader will catch.

Numbers also have costs. A value produced by a convenient method can look more certain than it is, and ecosystem services in particular are hard to price without biases that are known and rarely stated. Quantitative does not mean precise, and it does not mean complete.

## What a quantitative angle would add

A study published in this literature in 2022 reported that many practitioners believe circular practices do not always lead to sustainable outcomes, and asked for quantitative frameworks that make such trade-offs visible. That request seems to me the right one. It suggests a way of working:

- Say which quantity is claimed to improve, compared with what, over what period.
- Say what happens at the boundary: where do two objectives stop agreeing, and for which values of the controlling parameters?
- Report the answer under more than one defensible choice of weights, units and boundaries.
- Make the uncertainty part of the claim.
- Include a null. If the objectives never conflict in the system studied, that is a result too.

None of this replaces the work of those who measure social outcomes, interview practitioners or design policy. It gives their findings a place to be checked.
