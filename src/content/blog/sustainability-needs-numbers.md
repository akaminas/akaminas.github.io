Sustainability is usually discussed as a matter of values and commitments, and those matter. But whether something is sustainable gets decided by trade-offs, thresholds, delays and feedback, and each of those is a quantity. A claim with no quantity in it can't be wrong, which is a weakness.

## Terms that carry numbers

A handful of ideas from the field show how much hangs on a number.

The rebound effect is the observation that efficiency gains can be partly offset, or more than offset, by increased use. It is old. Jevons made the case for coal in The Coal Question (1865): better engines, more coal burned. Whether a more efficient process lowers total impact depends on how much extra use it invites, which is a number. Some arithmetic with made-up figures: a device uses 20 per cent less energy per use, so the expected saving is 20 per cent. If use rises by 12.5 per cent, energy falls by only 10 per cent (0.8 x 1.125 = 0.9), so half the expected saving is gone, a rebound of 50 per cent. If use rises by 25 per cent, 0.8 x 1.25 = 1 and the saving has vanished. Above that, total energy goes up. "There is a rebound effect" with no percentage next to it isn't yet a claim.

Decoupling comes in two kinds. In relative decoupling, impact per unit of output falls. In absolute decoupling, total impact falls. The first can hold while the second fails: if output grows 3 per cent a year and impact per unit falls 2 per cent a year, total impact still rises by about 0.9 per cent a year, and is 45 per cent higher after 40 years. Only total impact concerns the climate or the sea. How much is needed? With 3 per cent growth, impact per unit has to fall by more than 3/103, about 2.9 per cent a year, before the total even stops rising. To halve the total in 40 years it has to fall by 4.6 per cent a year, which leaves each unit of output with 15 per cent of today's impact at the end, while output is 3.3 times what it was. Without growth, 1.7 per cent a year would do the same job, so 3 per cent growth takes the required rate from 1.7 to 4.6.

The discount rate, which sets how future costs and benefits are weighed against present ones, can dominate every other assumption in an analysis. A cost of 100 arriving in 100 years is worth 37 today at a rate of 1 per cent, 5.2 at 3 per cent and 0.76 at 5 per cent. That is a factor of about 49 from one input. Much of the argument over the Stern Review in 2006 and 2007 was an argument about the rate it used. The rate belongs in the main text, not an appendix, with the result shown under an alternative.

System boundaries and functional units matter in the same way. A life-cycle assessment compares products per kilogram, per use or per year of service, and if you change the unit the ranking can change. A favourable result can reflect a convenient boundary. Here is a made-up case. A single-use item has 20 g of material at 3 kg of CO2e per kg, so 60 g of CO2e per item. A reusable one has 300 g of a material at 2 kg of CO2e per kg, so 600 g per item. Per kilogram of material the reusable one looks better (2 against 3). Per item made it is ten times worse. Per use, it depends on how many uses it gets: below 10 uses the single-use item wins, at 10 they tie, and at 50 the reusable one is at 12 g per use against 60. (I have left out washing, transport and disposal. Including them moves the break-even point but not the logic.) So the ranking is not a fact about the products until someone has said how long the reusable one lasts.

Planetary boundaries are global thresholds, set for the Earth system as a whole. A single firm can't be said to stay within them, and a firm-level model can't claim to. To say anything at that level you first need a rule for dividing the global budget among firms, countries or people, and the rule is a number with consequences. A firm with 2 per cent of world output and 0.5 per cent of world employment would be given a share four times larger if the budget were divided by output than by employment.

## Delays

A delay is a quantity too: how long. The simplest case is a stock with a steady inflow that loses a fixed share of itself each year. Take a pollutant removed at 2 per cent of the stock a year, a time scale of 50 years. Halve the inflow tomorrow and the stock heads for half its old level, but after 50 years it is only 63 per cent of the way there. Starting from 100, it is at 90.9 after 10 years, 83.5 after 20, 68.4 after 50 and 56.8 after 100 (made-up removal rate, simple exponential removal). Look at the stock after ten years and you see a fall of 9 per cent for a halving of the inflow. Whether that means the policy is working depends entirely on the 50, and a claim that doesn't mention it can't be checked.

## Commitment and practice

Suppose a survey of non-governmental organisations finds that commitment to sustainability is mostly conceptual, with only gradual efforts to turn it into practice. That is a cross-section: one moment, one set of organisations. It can't say how quickly stated commitment becomes practice, whether the gap is closing, or whether it stalls. Those are questions about rates, and answering them needs a model with the two quantities as state variables and data over time. A snapshot shows a gap, and only dynamics show whether it is narrowing.

To see how little the snapshot fixes, take a gap of 40 points between a commitment score and a practice score, and suppose practice closes a fixed share of the gap every year. At 20 per cent a year the gap is down to 5 points after about ten years. At 2 per cent it takes about a hundred. The survey is identical in the two cases. The same applies to many sustainability claims.

## Where the numbers are thin

There is a limit to this argument, and it's worth stating plainly. A 2021 review of social life cycle assessment, by Pollok and colleagues, describes it as less standardised and less mature than environmental assessment. A dynamic model of material and environmental flows does nothing about that gap. A study that models two of the three pillars should say so, since claiming three while modelling two is the kind of overreach a careful reader will catch.

Numbers also have costs. A value produced by a convenient method can look more certain than it is, and ecosystem services in particular are hard to price without biases that are known but rarely stated. Reporting a footprint as 12.37 kg when the inputs are only good to a factor of two is false precision that nobody can see. A quantitative claim can still be imprecise or incomplete, and it should say where.

## What a quantitative angle would add

Circular practices do not always lead to sustainable outcomes, and the way to see where they don't is a quantitative framework that makes the trade-offs visible. In practice I'd ask of any analysis, my own included:

- Which quantity is claimed to improve, compared with what, over what period? Impact per unit and total impact are different claims.
- What happens at the boundary: where do two objectives stop agreeing, and for which values of the controlling parameters? In the example above it is at 10 uses.
- Does the answer survive more than one defensible choice of weights, units, boundaries and discount rate?
- Is the uncertainty part of the claim?
- Is there a null? If the objectives never conflict in the system studied, that is a result too.

None of this replaces the work of those who measure social outcomes, interview practitioners or design policy. It gives their findings a place to be checked.
