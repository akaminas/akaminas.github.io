Sustainability is usually discussed as a matter of values and commitments, and those matter. But the questions that decide whether something is sustainable are about trade-offs, thresholds, delays and feedback, and each of those is quantitative. A claim with no quantity in it cannot be wrong, which is a weakness.

## Terms that carry numbers

A handful of ideas from the field show how much hangs on a number.

The rebound effect is the observation that efficiency gains can be partly offset, or more than offset, by increased use. It is old, and is associated with Jevons and coal in the nineteenth century. Whether a more efficient process lowers total impact depends on how much extra use it invites, which is a number.

Decoupling comes in two kinds. In relative decoupling, impact per unit of output falls. In absolute decoupling, total impact falls. The first can hold while the second fails: if output grows 3 per cent a year and impact per unit falls 2 per cent a year, total impact still rises by about 0.9 per cent a year, and is 45 per cent higher after 40 years. Only total impact concerns the climate or the sea.

The discount rate, which sets how future costs and benefits are weighed against present ones, can dominate every other assumption in an analysis. It belongs in the main text, not an appendix, with the result shown under an alternative.

System boundaries and functional units matter in the same way. A life-cycle assessment compares products per kilogram, per use or per year of service. Change the unit and the ranking of alternatives can change, and a favourable result can reflect a convenient boundary.

Planetary boundaries are global thresholds, set for the Earth system as a whole. A single firm cannot be said to stay within them, and a firm-level model cannot claim to.

## Commitment and practice

Suppose a survey of non-governmental organisations finds that commitment to sustainability is mostly conceptual, with only gradual efforts to turn it into practice. That is a cross-section: one moment, one set of organisations. It cannot say how quickly stated commitment becomes practice, whether the gap is closing, or whether it stalls. Those are questions about rates, and answering them needs a model with the two quantities as state variables and data over time. The same applies to many sustainability claims. A snapshot shows a gap, and only dynamics show whether it is narrowing.

## Where the numbers are thin

There is a limit to this argument, and it is worth stating plainly. A 2021 review of social life cycle assessment, by Pollok and colleagues, describes it as less standardised and less mature than environmental assessment. A dynamic model of material and environmental flows does not touch that gap. A study that models two of the three pillars should say so, since claiming three while modelling two is the kind of overreach a careful reader will catch.

Numbers also have costs. A value produced by a convenient method can look more certain than it is, and ecosystem services in particular are hard to price without biases that are known but rarely stated. A quantitative claim can still be imprecise or incomplete, and it should say where.

## What a quantitative angle would add

Circular practices do not always lead to sustainable outcomes, and the way to see where they do not is a quantitative framework that makes the trade-offs visible. That suggests a way of working:

- Say which quantity is claimed to improve, compared with what, over what period.
- Say what happens at the boundary: where do two objectives stop agreeing, and for which values of the controlling parameters?
- Report the answer under more than one defensible choice of weights, units and boundaries.
- Make the uncertainty part of the claim.
- Include a null. If the objectives never conflict in the system studied, that is a result too.

None of this replaces the work of those who measure social outcomes, interview practitioners or design policy. It gives their findings a place to be checked.
